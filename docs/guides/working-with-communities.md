# Working With Communities

## Participation should change something

Inviting people to a meeting, survey, or workshop does not by itself create shared agency. Participation has quality when people understand the scope, can influence a meaningful decision, receive the support needed to contribute, and can see what changed. If the decision is already fixed, communicate that honestly rather than describing outreach as co-design.

Use this suggested spectrum to name the intended level of influence:

> **Inform → Consult → Influence → Co-decide → Shared Governance**

This is an analytical model for project planning, not an official SD Standard scoring scale. Different parts of one project may sit at different levels. Information can be appropriate during an emergency; shared governance may be appropriate for a long-lived community platform. The ethical problem is promising more power than the process provides.

> **Central evaluation question:** What changed because people participated?

## Plan the participation conditions

- **Define the negotiable decision.** State what participants can change, what cannot change, who has final authority, and how disagreement will be handled.
- **Identify affected people, not only organized stakeholders.** Consider residents, workers, language communities, disabled people, children, migrants, informal groups, future maintainers, and people who may be harmed yet have little institutional access.
- **Make inclusion practical.** Provide accessible venues and digital tools, interpretation, childcare, transport, flexible timing, low-bandwidth options, plain language, and alternatives to public speaking.
- **Compensate expertise and labor.** Community members' time, cultural knowledge, translation, recruitment, facilitation, and review are project inputs. Budget payment and recognition early; do not make compensation depend on agreement.
- **Share problem definition.** Ask whether the brief names the right problem before requesting reactions to a proposed solution.
- **Address power.** Use independent facilitation when appropriate, publish decision rules, offer private channels, protect data, and prevent the loudest or best-resourced participant from standing in for everyone.
- **Allow conflict.** Consensus is not always possible or desirable. Record contested points, unequal risks, and unresolved issues without turning disagreement into a visual average.
- **Close the loop.** Return decisions in accessible formats, explain why suggestions were accepted or declined, identify next steps, and keep a route for correction.

Project participation is different from organizational participation capacity. A single co-design workshop does not establish long-term accountability. Capacity includes durable relationships, staff skills, governance arrangements, accessible systems, funding, and the ability to respond after launch.

## Examples

- A civic-information team asks tenants and housing advocates to define the questions a notice must answer, tests translations in context, and gives the tenant group approval authority over the final language.
- A community exhibition establishes a paid steering group, community-owned image permissions, a process for contested histories, and a plan for returning files and physical materials after closing.
- A climate-resilience campaign combines public information with neighborhood assemblies that allocate part of the implementation budget, then publishes decisions and progress.
- A participatory digital map supports anonymous and offline submissions, lets contributors set visibility and reuse permissions, and creates a community process for correction and removal.
- A public-service redesign reports not only attendance but which service rules, interface flows, or resource allocations changed because of participation.

## Questions for practice

- Why are we asking people to participate, and is there real room for influence?
- Who is affected but absent, and what barriers did the project create?
- Who owns recordings, photographs, stories, data, and resulting intellectual property?
- How are participants compensated, credited, protected, and able to withdraw?
- What happens when community priorities conflict with the commissioner’s goals?
- Who maintains the relationship and reports back after delivery?
- What changed in the problem, decision, resources, design, or governance?

## Evidence note

The value of clarity, inclusion, feedback, evaluation, and institutional capacity is supported by established public-participation guidance and longstanding scholarship. Evidence for any specific participation method is context-dependent, and more participation is not automatically better when scope, safety, or resources are inadequate. The five-level spectrum is proposed SD Standard guidance and should not be read as a certification ladder.
