# Making Sustainability Trade-offs

## Sustainability decisions are plural decisions

A design choice can improve one outcome while worsening another. A lightweight digital report may reduce printing but exclude people with limited connectivity. A local material may support place-based livelihoods but perform worse over a short service life. Accessibility can add file weight; durable construction can cost more initially; an efficient technology can depend on harmful labor conditions.

The task is not to collapse every value into one score. It is to define the decision, compare consequences across the four pillars, include the people who bear those consequences, and document why a compromise was accepted.

## Identify what is in tension

- **Competing objectives:** carbon, water, toxicity, biodiversity, accessibility, safety, cultural rights, fair work, affordability, and operational resilience may point in different directions.
- **Burden shifting:** an improvement can move impact between lifecycle stages, places, groups, or impact categories.
- **Plural valuation:** monetary cost and quantified environmental indicators are important but do not capture every cultural, relational, spiritual, or rights-based value.
- **Distribution:** totals can hide who receives benefits and who carries risk, unpaid labor, displacement, surveillance, or loss of access.
- **Constraints:** budgets, schedules, skills, contracts, and existing infrastructure are real, but should be recorded rather than presented as neutral laws.
- **Uncertainty:** unavailable data, contested methods, future behavior, and supplier claims can change the apparent preference.
- **Authority:** the person approving the design may not be the person who lives with its consequences.

## Values and Trade-offs Record

For a material decision, keep a short record alongside the brief or project file:

1. **What improves because of this decision?** Name the expected effect, affected group, timescale, and supporting evidence.
2. **What might become worse?** Include indirect, delayed, and rebound effects.
3. **Who receives the benefits and who carries the burdens?** Disaggregate where possible; do not use an average to erase unequal effects.
4. **Which alternatives were considered?** Include avoiding, reducing, reusing, delaying, piloting, and doing nothing—not only product substitutions.
5. **What evidence and values informed the final choice?** Record data quality, lived experience, applicable rights, dissent, assumptions, decision owner, and review date.

The record does not make a harmful choice acceptable. It makes the reasoning inspectable, gives stakeholders something specific to challenge, and creates a basis for learning when outcomes are known.

## Examples

- **Print versus digital:** Compare the actual audience, number and duration of uses, accessibility, devices, hosting, print run, paper and ink, distribution, archival need, and end of use. A hybrid option may be justified, but it is not automatically lower impact.
- **Local versus imported material:** “Local” may strengthen livelihoods and reduce transport, yet a distant supplier may offer verified labor safeguards, better durability, or a recovery system. Ask who defines local and which benefits remain in the community.
- **Accessibility versus file weight:** Do not frame access as a dispensable environmental cost. Test efficient implementation—semantic HTML, restrained media, captions and transcripts, optimized assets—then document any residual tension.
- **Affordability versus durability:** Compare total cost over the intended service period, repair availability, cash-flow constraints, and who bears replacement work. A long-lived option that only wealthy users can access can reproduce exclusion.
- **Efficient technology versus labor conditions:** Include supply-chain labor, content moderation, data annotation, maintenance, and disposal rather than limiting the comparison to operational energy.

## Facilitate the decision

State non-negotiable safeguards first: rights, safety, legal duties, informed consent, and avoidance of severe harm should not be traded away through a weighted score. Invite affected stakeholders early enough to change the options. Show indicators separately before aggregating them. Where values cannot be compared on a common scale, describe the conflict in plain language. Record minority or dissenting views and identify who made the final call.

Set a review trigger when evidence is weak: a pilot result, supplier audit, community decision, cost threshold, or measured usage pattern. Reopen the choice if the assumptions fail.

## Evidence note

Lifecycle assessment and materiality practices offer established methods for defining boundaries and identifying significant impacts, but results depend on data and value choices. Research on plural valuation strongly supports including diverse ways of valuing nature and human relationships. The five-question record is a proposed SD Standard practice; it has not been adopted as an official reporting or scoring requirement.
