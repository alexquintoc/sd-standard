# Cultural Sustainability in Practice

## Sustain capacity, not only representation

Cultural sustainability concerns people's ability to create, practice, transmit, adapt, contest, and govern culture over time. A project can display diverse images while weakening the language, livelihood, authority, or knowledge system that made those expressions possible.

> **Representing culture is not necessarily the same as sustaining cultural capacity.**

Communication designers therefore need to ask who has agency over meaning, access, continuity, and benefit—not only whether a finished artifact appears representative.

## Central concepts

- **Cultural agency and self-determination:** Communities and practitioners should be able to define significance, conditions of use, appropriate audiences, and future stewardship.
- **Living culture:** Heritage is not frozen source material. Practices and meanings are continually recreated by the people who maintain and transmit them.
- **Traditional and Indigenous knowledge:** Knowledge may have collective custodianship, customary access rules, sacred or restricted elements, and responsibilities that conventional copyright or open-data assumptions do not capture.
- **Continuity and transmission:** A publication or archive contributes more when it also strengthens language use, teaching, apprenticeships, community access, or the conditions for future practice.
- **Authenticity and mediation:** Every translation, photograph, label, interface, and visualization mediates. State who selected, translated, categorized, and interpreted the material.
- **Appropriation and extraction:** Permission to view is not necessarily permission to reproduce, commercialize, train a model, or detach material from context.
- **Linguistic diversity and localization:** Translation is more than substituting strings. Typography, reading direction, dialect, metaphor, voice, literacy, access technology, and community review can change the design system.
- **Economic sustainability:** Attribution without fair compensation can leave cultural practitioners visible but less able to continue their work.
- **Epistemic diversity:** Different knowledge systems may define evidence, relationships, categories, and authority differently. A database or chart should not force them into a single external ontology without negotiation.

## Apply cultural rights, consent, and care

Identify rights holders and knowledge holders before commissioning content. Agree on purpose, audiences, duration, territories, media, attribution, payment, storage, reuse, modification, commercial use, AI use, and withdrawal. Consent should be informed and ongoing when a project changes materially; a generic release form may be legally broad yet culturally inadequate.

Ask who controls the archive and source files after delivery. Use community-controlled metadata or culturally appropriate access labels where relevant. Provide corrections and takedown routes. Budget for translation, review, relationship-building, and future maintenance. If knowledge should not be digitized or publicly visualized, respect that boundary.

## Examples

- **Indigenous languages:** A public-service website supports the community's preferred orthography, fonts, audio, and review process; pays translators and language keepers; and funds updates rather than launching a one-time translated page.
- **Community archives:** Contributors set access and reuse conditions item by item. The archive returns preservation copies and catalog information to the community and does not assume that “open” is always ethical.
- **Traditional crafts:** An exhibition commissions practitioners as authors and advisors, explains living techniques rather than presenting an anonymous motif, and supports apprenticeships or sales on terms set with practitioners.
- **Data visualization:** A map combines quantitative measures with community-defined place names and relational values, shows data gaps, and avoids exposing sensitive sites.
- **Multilingual websites:** Navigation, search, forms, error messages, and support—not only campaign headlines—work in the selected languages, and analytics are reviewed for unequal completion rates.
- **AI-assisted content:** The team verifies provenance, obtains appropriate permission, checks stereotypes and language quality with affected people, discloses material AI use, and keeps a human author accountable for publication.

## Questions for practice

- Who has the authority to define, interpret, share, adapt, and withdraw this material?
- Does the project strengthen practice, transmission, livelihood, language, or governance after the campaign ends?
- Which cultural rules are invisible in the contract, platform, metadata, or license?
- Who is paid, credited, trained, and able to reuse the result?
- Does localization change the system or merely decorate an external structure?
- Could digitization, mapping, or AI expose restricted knowledge or separate it from its custodians?
- How will future community members correct the record?

## Evidence note

Community participation, cultural rights, living-heritage safeguarding, linguistic diversity, consent, and fair conditions for cultural workers are grounded in established international instruments. How these principles translate into a specific design process is context-dependent and should be determined with the relevant communities. “Cultural capacity” is used here as research-informed guidance, not as a new SD Standard score.
