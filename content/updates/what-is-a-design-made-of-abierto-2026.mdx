**MEXICO CITY — September 25, 2026 —** The Sustainable Design Standard (SD Standard), in collaboration with Mexico City–based design studio Arudeko, will present *¿De qué está hecho un diseño? / What Is a Design Made Of?* at the tenth edition of Abierto de Diseño CDMX. The interactive installation explores the environmental, social, cultural, and financial decisions behind design, using the project itself as a real-world test of the Standard’s four-pillar framework.

The installation asks visitors to look beyond the finished object and consider the network of decisions behind it: where materials come from, how much is produced, who makes it, whether people can access it, how it responds to local culture, how economic value is created and shared, and what happens when a design is no longer needed.

The project is particularly aligned with the Abierto’s tenth-edition theme, **Futuros**, which examines the role design can play in shaping the worlds being constructed today. The festival describes this year’s edition as an opportunity to reconsider issues ranging from living ecosystems and equity to diversity, inclusion, accessibility, and care.

> “Sustainable design is often understood primarily as a materials question, but every design is part of a much larger system,” said Alex Quinto, co-author of the Sustainable Design Standard. “We want designers to consider not only what something is made of, but who makes it, who can use it, what resources it depends on, what value it creates, and what happens next.”

## From checklist to design process

Developed over more than a decade with contributions from designers working across three continents, the Sustainable Design Standard began as a set of criteria for evaluating the impacts of visual communication projects.

The framework applies to areas including editorial design, exhibitions, signage, packaging, websites, interaction design, and motion graphics. Its criteria range from materials, energy, waste, and ecosystems to accessibility, labor, cultural context, community participation, artificial intelligence, fair compensation, and economic viability.

Rather than defining a single version of what constitutes “sustainable design,” the Standard is intended to help designers identify impacts, reduce harm, strengthen positive outcomes, and make tradeoffs more visible.

Since its public release earlier this year, the project has been expanding from a framework into a set of practical tools. [SDStandard.org](/) now includes a growing [Knowledge Base](/knowledge-base/) alongside tools for [evaluating projects](/calculator), [quickly identifying opportunities for improvement](/impact-snapshot), and [incorporating sustainability considerations into the earliest stages of a design brief](/brief-generator).

## Putting the Standard through its own test

The Abierto installation also serves as a real-world test of the framework itself. A central part of the installation was developed in close collaboration with Mexico City design studio Arudeko, bringing material experimentation, textile production, artisan knowledge, and local making directly into the design process. The collaboration allowed the project to examine sustainability not only through the materials selected, but through the relationships, skills, labor, and cultural knowledge involved in producing them.

Decisions involved in producing the exhibition—from material quantities and printing to fabrication, energy, labor, accessibility, transportation, cost, reuse, and end-of-life planning—are being considered through the same criteria that the Standard asks other designers to apply.

The intention is not to present the installation as a “perfectly sustainable” object.

Instead, it is being treated as an experiment in making design decisions, evidence, uncertainties, and compromises more visible.

> “We see design and production as more than a question of materials. They are also about relationships, skills, knowledge, traditions, and creating value within a community. This project gave us a chance to bring those ideas into a broader conversation about sustainable design.”
> — Denisse, Co-founder. Arudeko

Visitors will be able to encounter the Standard through its four pillars, explore individual criteria, and use digital tools that extend the experience beyond the exhibition.

The installation will also contribute to the next stage of the SD Standard’s development: testing the framework with designers and real projects to understand which criteria are most useful, which are difficult to apply, and what kinds of evidence designers can realistically collect. ## About Abierto de Diseño CDMX

Abierto de Diseño CDMX celebrates its tenth edition in 2026, bringing together exhibitions, installations, experiences, and conversations that explore design in its many forms. This year’s program uses the idea of Futuros as a framework for examining how contemporary design practices are imagining and constructing possible futures. The 2026 edition runs September 25 through October 4, with Centro Nacional de las Artes serving as a principal venue and programming extending across the city.

## About the Sustainable Design Standard

The Sustainable Design Standard (SD Standard) is an open framework that helps designers identify and assess the environmental, social, cultural, and financial impacts of their work.

Developed with international input, the Standard provides practical criteria for questioning design decisions, exploring alternatives, documenting evidence, reducing negative impacts, and strengthening positive outcomes.

Its long-term goal is to make sustainability assessment a more routine part of professional design practice—from the brief and design process through production, use, and end of life.

## About Arudeko

Arudeko is a Mexico City-based design studio that explores the contemporary possibilities of artisanal production.

Its practice bridges traditional craftsmanship with contemporary design, material experimentation, and new application contexts. Through collaborations with artisans and workshops across Mexico, the studio develops textiles and objects that translate local techniques and know-how into new scales, languages, and settings.

Arudeko seeks to expand the scope of handmade work, bringing artisanal production into residential, architectural, and hospitality projects while remaining deeply connected to material knowledge, identity, and the stories behind each process.
